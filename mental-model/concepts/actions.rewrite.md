# Actions, authority and observations

An Execution asks the world to do things it cannot do itself: publish a file, charge a card, hand a question to a person, create another Execution. The publishing and charging and asking happen outside the [Kernel](core.md#kernel)'s process. But the *decision* to attempt them, the *permission* under which the attempt runs, and the *record* of what happened flow through the Kernel — and the vocabulary on this page exists so that a reader can tell those three things apart in cases where they otherwise blur together.

The central discipline the vocabulary encodes is that the Kernel is not an enforcer beyond the moment of admission. Before admission, it can refuse a request that fails validation, authority, policy or consent, and the request never runs. From the moment an admitted request leaves a trusted adapter, the Kernel becomes a *recorder*: something happened, or may have happened, and its job is to write down what can actually be established, including that nothing can. Every design choice below follows from that split, and it is worth stating up front because several of the vocabulary distinctions look pedantic until you realise they are the difference between a record that acknowledges the truth and one that pretends to know more than it does.

## A first example

Follow one publication end to end. The Runtime drafts the weekly report and proposes an [Outcome](core.md#outcome) containing an **Effect**: "publish this report through the `publish_report` operation, with argument `{reportRef: r17}`." The Kernel [accepts the Outcome](../mechanisms/execution-cycle.md#outcome-acceptance); the Effect becomes an accepted **logical action** with an accepted **intent**. Before anything runs, **admission** checks the action against the Execution's **authority**, current **policy** and — because `publish_report` requires it — a person's **exact consent** for this specific publication. Once admitted, a trusted adapter sends the request; the service acts, or may act, and eventually authenticated evidence arrives back. **Settlement** records what that evidence says about this particular attempt, and the Runtime learns the result in a later Activation as a result [Event](core.md#event). Every term set in bold above is defined precisely below, in the same order.

The terms fall into three passes over that story. **The action itself:** the operation the request invokes, the Effect that proposes it, the logical action it becomes on acceptance, the admission that authorises an attempt, the settlement that records what came back. **The gate at admission:** the principal who is asking, the authority that bounds what they may do, and the exact consent that binds a person's yes to one unchangeable action. **What sits alongside actions:** exposure and mediation (visibility versus governance), the pair of controls — withdrawal and compensation — that apply to a request already in flight, and the vocabulary for output, which is what a Runtime *produces* rather than *requests*.

## Operation

A Runtime cannot ask the Kernel to do something unnamed. Before any mediated action runs, the thing it invokes must already exist as a named, versioned contract: an **operation**. Pre-declaration is not administrative overhead; it is the anchor that makes admission, consent and settlement possible at all. Permission is checked against the operation, a person's approval pins the operation, and later evidence is interpreted through the operation. Without a stable target for those three decisions to bind to, admission would be validating shape without meaning, consent would be approving a plan without a specific commitment, and settlement would be receiving evidence about no particular request.

An operation contract has five parts, and it is worth pausing on each — because the temptation is to treat only the first two as important, and the last three are where the interesting failures live.

1. **Identity.** A stable name that survives every revision, so `publish_report` stays `publish_report` when its argument schema moves from revision 2 to revision 3. Admission, approval and evidence all reference this name plus a pinned revision; the pair is what "the same operation" means across time.
2. **Input and output schema.** The shape of arguments and results — `publish_report` takes `{reportRef}` and returns `{publishedAt}`. This is the least surprising part of the contract, and the most commonly assumed to be sufficient.
3. **Supported schema features.** Which parts of that shape are actually enforced: whether an unknown property on the argument is refused or silently dropped, whether a missing field is substituted with a default. Two schemas that name the same fields can still disagree here, and the disagreement is the difference between "the request I approved" and "the request the adapter sent." The concrete validator and its enforced subset are chosen by later work; this part deliberately does not name a validator.
4. **Exact input meaning.** What a valid-looking argument actually asks for. `reportRef` must name the exact reviewed revision, not any string of the right shape. A schema check only confirms shape; without the meaning, a person approving the action cannot know what they are approving. This part matters most where a human is in the loop, and it is the reason [exact consent](#exact-consent) binds validated arguments rather than a shape they conformed to.
5. **Result-certainty behaviour.** What an attempt can ever prove afterwards. Some operations can distinguish "definitely failed" from "may have run"; others cannot, because the service offers no query and no idempotent retry. That declaration decides which positions along [settlement's four dimensions](#settlement-and-reconciliation) an attempt can ever reach, and an operation with no way to establish outcome can never move past "unknown" — however the Kernel records it.

An operation sits deliberately apart from three neighbouring contracts it can be confused with, because each of those is a contract of a different kind. A [Resource](state.md#resource-binding-and-attachment) is *acted on* rather than called: writes and reads flow to it under a schema, but you do not invoke it. A **Service** is the private implementation behind one or more selected operations; the same service can back several operations, and none of that is visible at the operation contract. A [Skill](roles.md#skill-and-package) packages instructions and assets rather than exposing a callable, and its manifest *requests* operations rather than being one. "Invocable" is what sets an operation apart from all three.

A model chooses from names it can read, and human-friendly labels help. But permission and evidence need identities that do not drift. So an operation keeps its identity when shown under a friendlier label. A [projection](roles.md#projection-and-invocation-binding) may display `publish_report` as "Publish this week's report," and the caller still invoked the same operation. That matters because the label can change between requests, while a late reply must still resolve to the operation the caller actually saw, not to whatever the catalog offers under that label now.

Operations and [Activations](core.md#activation) vary independently. One Activation can propose many operations in its Outcome, and many Activations can name the same operation over time. Operation identity is a property of the world outside the Execution; Activation identity is a property of one exchange within it. Nothing about an operation is scoped to a particular Activation or Runtime attempt.

## Effect

An **Effect** is a proposal for one Kernel-mediated action, carried in an [Outcome](core.md#outcome). Nothing about an Effect is action yet; it is what the Runtime asks the Kernel to record, and it becomes real only when the [Outcome is accepted](../mechanisms/execution-cycle.md#outcome-acceptance).

Effects cover more than service invocations. Invoking a service and reading governed data are the two shapes that name an [operation](#operation): the Effect points at an existing operation by identity and pinned revision, and the Effect's arguments are validated against that operation's schema. Three other shapes carry no operation reference. [Requesting human input](../mechanisms/communication.md#human-input-requests), creating a [child](operations.md#child-and-ownership) and sending a [message](operations.md#message-request-and-correlation) each have their own proposal shape and their own admission rules; none of them names an operation, because none of them invokes a callable in the sense operations describe. What makes all five kinds Effects is that they cross a boundary the Kernel is asked to govern — unlike an internal function call or a model API call, which stay entirely inside the Runtime.

An Effect is not an [Emission](#emission-result-and-output-obligation). The distinction is subtle in prose but sharp in meaning: an **Emission** is something the Runtime has already produced and asks the Kernel to record as accepted output. An **Effect** is something the Runtime wants done. Output that has to reach a specific external recipient is not an Emission — it is an Effect that invokes a channel-adapter operation whose implementation performs the send. The Kernel holds output; the world receives it only through a separate authorised action.

Within one Outcome, the Runtime identifies its own Effects by a **proposal key** — a local, stable name whose only purpose is to let the same Outcome refer to its own Effect from elsewhere, for example from a [wait](core.md#wait-subscription-and-generation) that depends on that Effect's result. On acceptance the proposal key becomes bound to an **Effect ID**: the immutable logical request identity within the Execution. The Effect ID is not the identity of any physical send. A single Effect can be attempted more than once at the physical level, and its logical identity stays constant across those attempts — which is exactly what lets settlement evidence from a second physical attempt refine what the Kernel knows about the first, without being recorded as a different action.

## Logical action and intent

Acceptance of an Effect creates a **logical action**: an immutable action record within the Execution. The record identifies which operation the action invokes (if it names one), which arguments it carries, and which Effect ID it belongs to. Nothing about this record can change afterwards; correction takes the form of new action evidence appended under the same ID, never edits to what has already been consumed by the Runtime.

Alongside that record, acceptance also installs an **intent**. An intent is an accepted obligation to perform or route the specified work later. It is not evidence that the work has already happened, and it is not evidence that it has even been attempted; it is the Kernel's commitment that this Execution is on record as owing it. The gap between the intent and any attempt is the whole point of separating them. [Accepting the Outcome](../mechanisms/execution-cycle.md#outcome-acceptance) commits the Runtime's progress and its intents in one atomic decision, so a crash between acceptance and the first attempt leaves a recorded obligation the Kernel can find and reconcile, rather than a lost request that no one remembers making. Without the intent, a caller retrying its Outcome after losing the acknowledgment could easily create a second obligation for what was meant to be one action.

Throughout this page, "action" without qualification means mediated work — work that flows through the Kernel-governed admission and settlement path. Native tools that a Runtime calls directly, using powers the deployment gave it, are also actions in the ordinary English sense; when the distinction has to be made, they are called **native actions** or **ambient actions**. The difference matters most under [exposure and mediation](#exposure-and-mediation) below.

## Admission and physical action attempt

An accepted intent is a request that exists. Whether it may actually run is decided next, in a separate step.

**Admission** is the ordered decision that authorises a concrete physical attempt to execute a logical action. It runs three checks at once: the Execution's [authority](#principal-and-authority), the current policy (which may have narrowed since the Outcome was accepted), and — for operations that require it — a valid, applicable [exact consent](#exact-consent). The order matters, and the [ordering rules](../mechanisms/authority.md) live on the authority-mechanism page.

Admission records the authorised attempt under **current dispatch ownership**: the responsibility to actually send this attempt is held by a specific dispatcher at a specific moment, and admission fences it. A **physical action attempt** is one invocation of the authorised logical action through a trusted adapter. Admission can precede sending — the attempt is authorised and recorded before the bytes leave the process — but admission cannot prove receipt: the fact that the Kernel decided to send does not mean the service ever received the request. This is the first place the recorder-not-enforcer split shows up in the vocabulary. After admission, the Kernel can only observe what comes back.

The dispatch ownership admission fences is deliberately separate from the [writer epoch](identity.md#writer-epoch) that fences Outcome acceptance for the Runtime attempt that proposed the action. The Runtime attempt and the action dispatch have different lifecycles: a takeover might replace the Runtime attempt long after its Outcome was accepted and its Effect intents were admitted for dispatch, and it would be wrong for a Runtime-side fence to invalidate a dispatch already authorised under the old attempt. Independent fencing is what keeps two questions separate — which Runtime attempt may write progress, and which dispatch may perform this specific physical attempt.

## Settlement and reconciliation

After a physical attempt, the Kernel needs to record what happened. It gets that record in one of two ways, and the difference between them is worth stating explicitly because they answer different questions.

**Settlement** accepts authenticated evidence for a particular action attempt and records its observation for the Runtime. The evidence comes back through a trusted path — the same adapter that sent the request, or a callback that authenticates against the same account — and is accepted or refused on its own terms. A payload from an unauthenticated origin is not settlement evidence, however plausible it looks.

**Reconciliation** queries or inspects existing external work through a trusted path to resolve uncertainty. Reconciliation is not blind re-execution. When the answer to "did the publish run?" went missing, reconciliation asks the service — through an authorised read — rather than publishing again to find out. Re-execution to discover status is exactly the anti-pattern this vocabulary exists to prevent: publishing twice to figure out whether the first publish happened is not measurement, it is a second publication.

What settlement records is not a single "status" — it is a position along four independent dimensions, and collapsing them loses real facts every time.

| Dimension | Meaning and example |
|---|---|
| **Request disposition** | What may happen next for this request: waiting for approval, denied, withdrawn, no more attempts. |
| **Attempt evidence / certainty** | What is known externally about whether the attempt ran: no attempt made, may have run, observed success, definite failure, unknown. |
| **Result validity** | Whether the returned value satisfies the result contract, even if the action ran and produced something. |
| **Responsibility / obligation** | Who still owes settlement or required results: this Execution, a named durable owner (say, a child the work was transferred to), or an explicit policy abandonment. |

These are conceptual dimensions, not a mandated enum cross-product. An operation whose result-certainty declaration cannot distinguish "the write happened, and the receipt body is malformed" from "we cannot tell whether the write happened" is honestly limited to fewer positions on those axes, and the vocabulary lets the record say so.

Two of the more consequential things that collapsing dimensions hides:

- **An invalid result can coexist with proven external success.** The service confirmed the publication ran; the response body it returned is unusable for the Runtime's next step. That is one accepted attempt with observed success along the certainty axis and a validity failure along the validity axis. A single "status" field cannot say both, yet the Runtime must act differently on each — the report exists in the world, and the fact that the Runtime cannot use the confirmation does not change that fact.
- **Stopping retries changes disposition, not certainty.** An operator's "stop trying" moves the request to a terminal disposition. The Kernel now knows what this Execution will do next; it does not know whether the service ran the previous attempt. Acknowledging an unknown observation does not remove the obligation, and neither does refusing further tries.

The dimension that most often gets swept away in careless prose is certainty. "Unknown" is a disposition, not a failure; it is what the Kernel honestly records when the evidence to say more is absent. A design that turns unknown into failure at the first opportunity is treating absence of a receipt as proof of non-execution — which is exactly what leads to duplicate charges, duplicate publications and duplicate notifications when reconciliation would have found the receipt on a second look.

## Principal and authority

Admission asked whether a request is permitted. The next three sections define what "permitted" is measured against: who is asking, what bound applies, and when a human must decide this one.

A **principal** is an authenticated application identity — a user, a service, or an acting-on-behalf-of identity — with tenant or application scope where applicable. Principals belong to the application's identity domain, not the Kernel's; the Kernel receives a principal through authenticated ingress and records it, but does not mint principals of its own. An Execution is not a principal. The work has an authority binding, but the work does not authenticate as anyone: the principal on whose behalf it acts was named at creation and cannot be inferred from a payload later, however that payload spells `user_id`.

**Authority** is the upper bound of Kernel-mediated operations and resources available to an Execution. It is a ceiling, not a set of guarantees: an Execution with authority to invoke `publish_report` may still be refused by policy on any given attempt. The ceiling exists so that policy has something concrete to narrow.

**Policy** decides whether a concrete request is permitted right now, and can narrow authority further. Policy is where fresh facts enter — whether the account has been suspended since the Outcome was accepted, whether an approval has been withdrawn, whether the argument's target has been added to a blocklist. Local policy is checked against just-accepted local state with immediate consistency; remote policy is checked against a service whose freshness is a separate question, and the [ordering rules](../mechanisms/authority.md#order-revocation-against-admission) fix how a race between revocation and admission is resolved.

A **grant** records authority and, when applicable, the delegation constraints that let a parent Execution pass some of its authority to a [child](operations.md#child-and-ownership). Grants are the durable form of authority: they name what has been given, by whom, under what constraints, and with what expiry. Trusted records can suffice within one trust domain; signatures authenticate origin but do not prove current permission — the grant may still have been revoked.

**Delegation** gives a child only what survives three simultaneous limits: what was requested for it, what the parent was allowed to pass on, and what current policy permits now. A parent that may email exactly one address cannot give a child the power to email any address by asking for it — the child gets that one address, or nothing. Being created by someone never by itself grants what the creator has, because ancestry is responsibility, not permission.

## Exact consent

Policy decides most requests without a person. A few actions are consequential enough that a human must decide this one — and the decision has to bind to the action that will actually run, not to the plan that produced it. Every constraint in this section exists to close the gap between what a person was shown and what the adapter sends.

**Exact consent** is one human's yes to one unchangeable action. It binds four things together, all of which must survive from approval to admission:

- The **specific action** — which Execution, which logical action ID. An approval for an action is not an approval for a similar action in a different Execution or under a later logical ID.
- The **exact request** — validated arguments under a pinned operation version. Not the pre-validation input a model produced, and not the post-approval input a hook might rewrite — the same bytes admission will check.
- The **real-world targets** — the account the action touches, the resource revision or immutable content digest where it applies. An approval to publish "this draft" binds to the specific revision the person saw; a later edit to that draft, however small, is not the thing they approved.
- The **approval itself** — who may approve, and how long the yes lasts. An approval from a person who is not eligible under policy is not consent, and an expired approval is not consent even if it has not been withdrawn.

Two things that look like consent but are not: ordinary conversational feedback, and standing intent. A user saying "sure, go ahead" in a message stream is untyped text the Runtime may act on however it likes; it may inform a proposal, but it does not settle the [exact consent](#exact-consent) contract for any particular action. A stored preference — "this user usually approves publications from this template" — is standing intent that policy may take into account; it does not by itself approve any concrete publication.

Approving a plan is not approving its eventual actions. "Plan A: draft, review, publish" is a workflow proposal. Publication under Plan A is a concrete action against a specific recipient and payload, and requires its own consent bound to that recipient and payload. The rejection of plan-as-consent is not administrative caution; it is the recognition that a plan can produce many different concrete actions, and a person who agreed to the plan did not thereby agree to whichever one the system eventually generated.

## Exposure and mediation

Two words describe what the Kernel does and does not do about visibility and control of actions, and the difference between them accounts for a large fraction of the confusion that arises around what "governed" means.

**Exposure** means filtered visibility of authorised operation metadata or callable choices — showing a model that `publish_report` exists, describing what its arguments look like, ranking it above or below other operations for a search query. Exposure is not a grant and not a mandatory durable object. Showing a model that `publish_report` exists does not authorise any particular publication; showing a *different* set of operations to a *different* Execution does not create any new authority relationship, only a different presentation of the same catalog.

**Mediation** means the specific action path goes through Kernel admission and settlement. A mediated action is one the Kernel decides on and records. Mediation makes it possible for the Kernel to say what was requested, who authorised it, when it was attempted and what came back — for the paths that flow through it, and for those paths only.

**Ambient action** or **native action** uses powers provided directly by the Runtime's host or native system: a filesystem write from a process that has filesystem access, an HTTP call from a Runtime whose network is not restricted. These actions are not mediated; the Kernel has no admission record for them and no settlement record either. Telemetry that observes such an action — a wrapper library that logs every outbound HTTP request — does not mediate it, and does not prevent it. Only mediation itself or [physical isolation](operations.md#trusted-execution) provided by the deployment can. This is one of the most important places to keep the boundary honest: a claim that "the Kernel governs all actions" is only true for the mediated ones, and the deployment decides which ones those are.

## Withdrawal and compensation

Two controls apply after a request exists, and they act on different things. One acts on the request's future; the other acts on the world the request has already changed. Confusing them tends to produce prose that sounds like it can undo an external side effect, and no prose can.

**Withdrawal** is an explicit control preventing future admission of a named request. Withdrawal targets a specific logical action ID and takes effect atomically against admission: an admission decision that observes the withdrawal will refuse the attempt, and an admission decision that has already been made stands. A queued correction message from a user — "actually, don't publish that" — is *not* withdrawal, because it is untyped input the Runtime may or may not read, on its own schedule. Withdrawal is a Kernel-level control the application invokes on the specific intent it wants to stop.

**Compensation** is a new authorised action intended to counter an earlier action. Compensation is not rollback of external history. A retraction notice is a second publication, not the unpublishing of the first; a refund is a second transfer, not the undoing of the original charge. The external world does not forget what happened; it only accepts new events. Compensation names those new events as related to the ones they counter, so that a Runtime or an operator can reason about the resulting state — but the compensation itself flows through the same admission and settlement path as any other action, and its evidence stands alongside the original's rather than replacing it.

The pairing is worth spelling out because the temptation is to imagine one mechanism that "cancels" an action across both dimensions. There is no such mechanism. Withdrawal stops what has not yet happened. Compensation authorises something new that responds to what has. Anything that claimed to do both would either be silently unable to affect one of them, or would be conflating them in a way that hides real facts about the state of the world.

## Emission, result and output obligation

Not everything a Runtime produces is a request. The remaining terms name output — content the Kernel *records* but does not *perform*.

An **Emission** is accepted non-terminal output from an Outcome. The Runtime says, in effect, "here is a chunk of the report so far"; on Outcome acceptance the Emission is assigned a stable identity and retained under the Execution's output obligation. Emissions can be observed by authorised readers and can be replayed for a reader that disconnects and reconnects. Nothing about accepting an Emission *sends* it anywhere: reading an Emission through an authorised subscription is a different act from having the Emission delivered to a channel.

A **terminal result** is output accepted with completion. It arrives at the moment the Runtime proposes `complete` in its Outcome and the Kernel accepts that Outcome. Like an Emission, a terminal result is retained and observable; unlike an Emission, there is exactly one per Execution, and its acceptance is bound to the Execution's transition to `COMPLETED`.

**Provisional output** is unaccepted diagnostic or streaming content — stdout tokens from a native run that is still in progress, for example. Provisional output can be shown to observers when the presentation clearly labels it unaccepted, but it can never certify consent, action success or a terminal result. A token stream and an accepted Emission look identical in a terminal, and this labelled distinction is exactly what keeps a mid-attempt fragment from being mistaken for confirmed output when the attempt is later replaced.

An **output obligation** makes accepted Emissions and terminal results available for authorised, retention-bounded observation and replay. This is a *pull* contract: the Kernel guarantees availability, not delivery. An older phrase used for the same obligation — "publication intent" — is misleading, because it suggests automatic public disclosure or push-to-channel behaviour, and the current term is the accurate one. External delivery to a user, another service or a channel is a separate application-adapter responsibility; if that delivery is required for business success, the Runtime must request the delivery through its own [Effect](#effect) and observe the result, rather than assume that accepted output has traveled anywhere on its own.
